# Motion Grammar

> Motion is not decoration.
>
> It is the visible expression of judgment unfolding through time.

---

# Purpose

Motion Grammar defines how meaning unfolds through time inside Stencil.

It is not a guide to animation styles, easing curves, or transition effects. Those belong to implementation.

Motion Grammar exists to answer a different question:

> **What is movement allowed to mean?**

Every movement inside Stencil is interpreted as evidence.

A pause means something.

A correction means something.

A disappearance means something.

A continuation means something.

If those meanings are inconsistent with the product's philosophy, the interface begins lying even while its copy remains truthful.

Motion therefore becomes part of the editorial language rather than a decorative layer applied after the design has been completed.

If Visual Grammar determines **what becomes visible**, Motion Grammar determines **how that visibility unfolds**.

---

# Governing Question

How should meaning change through time?

Not:

How should an interface animate?

---

# First Principles

Motion exists to make judgment visible.

Movement should never exist simply because something changed state.

Every movement must communicate editorial intent.

Removing a movement should reduce understanding.

If removing a movement changes nothing about the founder's understanding, that movement was decoration rather than language.

Motion therefore belongs to meaning before aesthetics.

---

# The Five Laws

## Law 1 — Tempo

### Principle

> **Tempo is variance, not duration.**

Stencil is not slow.

Stencil redistributes time.

Observation is quick.

Judgment receives time.

Correction is decisive.

Resolution rests.

Its rhythm is governed by rubato rather than mechanical cadence.

---

### Why

Deliberateness is frequently misunderstood as slowness.

Editors do not work slowly.

They scan rapidly.

They pause.

Then they make one decisive mark.

The identity comes from the uneven distribution of time rather than prolonged movement.

Equal timing produces mechanical rhythm.

Unequal timing produces editorial rhythm.

---

### Design Consequence

Stop tuning the duration of individual transitions in isolation.

Start budgeting a fixed span of time across an entire sequence.

The unit of design is the sequence, not the transition.

If one beat receives more time, another must visibly receive less.

Time is allocated rather than added.

A transition submitted for review without its surrounding sequence is not reviewable.

The central design review question becomes:

> **What did this beat take time from?**

If the answer is:

> Nothing. I simply made it longer.

The law has been violated.

---

### Counterexample

Uniform slowness.

Every transition is equally deliberate.

Nothing feels important because nothing has been given more attention than anything else.

The interface becomes heavy rather than thoughtful.

---

## Law 2 — Weight

### Principle

> **Weight is consequence, not force.**

A mark carries authority because it changes the record.

Not because it appears visually aggressive.

---

### Why

Force communicates emotion.

Weight communicates commitment.

Stencil behaves like an editor rather than a disciplinarian.

Every committed mark carries equal authority.

Authority comes from permanence rather than pressure.

---

### Design Consequences

Movement should never exaggerate importance through violence.

Authority comes from changing the state of the work.

Not from scale.

Not from bounce.

Not from overshoot.

Not from dramatic acceleration.

A correction should feel heavier because it alters the record, not because it arrives theatrically.

The designer therefore reviews consequence before emphasis.

---

### Counterexample

A correction that slams into place.

A dramatic animation communicating emotional certainty instead of editorial certainty.

The interface appears confident through force rather than evidence.

---

## Law 3 — Continuity

### Principle

> **Continuity governs causality, not movement.**

Stencil acts in discrete editorial units.

One action never guarantees the next.

---

### Why

Continuous movement leaks the ending.

If observation inevitably flows into correction, correction has already been decided.

Judgment requires genuine uncertainty.

After reading, it must remain possible that nothing happens.

Each action therefore completes before another begins.

The join between actions becomes evidence.

---

### Design Consequences

Prefer cuts over morphs.

Prefer completed actions over continuous transformations.

Avoid transitions that imply the outcome before judgment has occurred.

Every action should earn the next rather than predict it.

A designer reviews relationships between actions rather than smoothness between states.

---

### Counterexample

Shared element transitions.

Continuous morphing.

Crossfades that erase the distinction between before and after.

Movement that suggests inevitability instead of judgment.

## Law 4 — Persistence

### Principle

> **Stencil preserves every committed mark and may withdraw only its own uncommitted proposal.**

Persistence is not the refusal to delete.

Persistence is the discipline of preserving evidence.

Committed work becomes part of the record.

Only provisional work may disappear.

---

### Why

The founder owns the work.

Stencil owns only its suggestions.

That asymmetry is intentional.

Stencil may reject a sentence.

It may annotate it.

It may challenge it.

It may never erase the founder's reasoning from history.

Committed history becomes evidence.

Evidence is never rewritten.

The only exception is a proposal made by Stencil that has not yet been accepted.

Because it never entered the record, it may be withdrawn without falsifying history.

---

### Design Consequences

Strike-throughs remain legible.

Rejected alternatives remain referenceable.

Revision history becomes part of understanding rather than interface clutter.

A designer should ask:

> **Is this removal deleting history or withdrawing a proposal?**

If it deletes committed reasoning, the action violates the grammar.

---

### Counterexample

Undo systems that silently replace history.

Interfaces that overwrite previous thinking as though it never existed.

Clean surfaces that communicate certainty by hiding the reasoning that produced it.

---

## Law 5 — Completion

### Principle

> **Completion does not end the work. It ends the surface's editability.**

A completed surface changes status.

It no longer exists to receive marks.

It exists to be referenced.

---

### Why

Completion is frequently mistaken for finality.

Stencil rejects that assumption.

Finished work does not become permanent truth.

It becomes stable evidence.

Future work may cite it.

Future work may challenge it.

Future work may supersede it.

Future work may not silently rewrite it.

Completion therefore governs the status of work rather than its importance.

---

### Design Consequences

Every working surface should have a recognisable transition into reference.

Open surfaces invite intervention.

Reference surfaces invite citation.

The transition between those two states should be visible.

Future changes occur by opening a new surface rather than quietly altering an old one.

---

### Counterexample

Editing a completed decision without leaving evidence that it changed.

History appears unchanged even though the underlying decision has moved.

The interface claims continuity while destroying provenance.

# Structural Invariants

Motion Grammar is fractal.

The same relationships repeat across scales.

A pause inside a correction sequence, a completed worksheet in a workshop, and a frozen chapter in the repository are not different ideas.

They are the same operation expressed at different levels.

Motion Grammar therefore governs more than animation.

It governs how work changes state.

Every law should survive changes of scale.

---

## Tempo

At the beat level:

Time is redistributed across observation, judgment, correction, and rest.

At the session level:

Time is redistributed across questioning, silence, discovery, and commitment.

The rhythm changes.

The law does not.

---

## Continuity

At the interaction level:

Corrections occur as discrete editorial acts.

At the product level:

Screens complete before others begin.

At the project level:

Workshops complete before the next workshop begins.

Nothing silently morphs into its successor.

---

## Persistence

At the page level:

Rejected sentences remain visible.

At the workshop level:

Reasoning remains available.

At the repository level:

History is superseded rather than rewritten.

---

## Completion

At the surface level:

A page becomes a reference.

At the chapter level:

A chapter becomes stable.

At the repository level:

Canonical knowledge becomes something later work cites rather than edits.

Completion changes the status of work rather than its value.

---

### Why this matters

When the same laws operate across interaction, documentation, and implementation, contributors learn one way of thinking instead of several unrelated conventions.

Consistency emerges from recurring relationships rather than repeated styling.

---

# Misinterpretations

## "Stencil should feel slow."

No.

Stencil redistributes time.

Slowness is not the goal.

Editorial rhythm is.

---

## "Discrete means abrupt."

No.

Discrete describes the relationship between actions, not their visual harshness.

Actions may be calm.

They may not imply one another.

---

## "Persistence means nothing may disappear."

No.

Only committed history persists.

Unaccepted proposals may be withdrawn.

History may never be silently rewritten.

---

## "Completion means finished."

No.

Completion changes a surface from editable to referenceable.

Work continues.

Editing moves elsewhere.

---

# Design Review Checklist

Before approving any motion ask:

- Does this movement communicate judgment?
- Could this movement be removed without changing meaning?
- Does the movement reveal the ending too early?
- Does it preserve evidence?
- Is authority created through consequence rather than force?
- Has the surface genuinely changed state?
- Is this sequence allocating time or simply adding duration?
- Is committed history preserved?

If these questions cannot be answered, the motion has not yet been designed.

# Closing

Motion Grammar is not a set of instructions about how things should move.

It is a theory of what movement is permitted to claim.

Every law in this chapter turns on the same distinction: whether something is still in play or has already been placed.

Tempo lies when time is spent evenly rather than according to judgment.

Weight lies when force is substituted for consequence.

Continuity lies when one action foretells another.

Persistence lies when history disappears.

Completion lies when a finished surface pretends it can still be edited.

These are not aesthetic failures.

They are false statements made in time rather than in words.

A product can be entirely truthful in its copy and entirely dishonest in its behaviour.

Carry this into Scene Direction.

Every decision about where attention moves, what is withheld, what is revealed, and when it is revealed asks exactly the same question at a larger scale.

Motion is a claim.

Every claim made through motion must be defensible by evidence.

Compose a sequence the way an editor defends a mark:

with the join visible,

the judgment exposed,

and the ending genuinely unwritten until it arrives.
